The 2/20 Waterfall: How Carried Interest Actually Splits an AIF's Money (and What to Negotiate)

The LP economics layer over AIFs: how the 'two and twenty' ladder really works — management fee, 8% hurdle, 100% catch-up and 80/20 split — with a worked ₹500 Cr fund example; why the hurdle and catch-up are the terms that actually move, and what a clawback does when the fund misses.

DhanPedia · Author Chirag Singhal · Verified 2026-10-18

The AIF category selection guide told you which category to pick and showed the fee-decay math on a one-year basis. This article goes one level deeper, into the part of the fund document (the PPM / LPA) that actually decides how much of the fund's money ends up with you: the distribution waterfall. "2 and 20" is not one number — it is four numbers, in a fixed order, and two of them (the hurdle and the catch-up) are where your real negotiating power is.

<2>The four levers, in the order the money flows

Every Indian Category I / II AIF runs some version of the same ladder:

  1. Management fee — the GP's quarterly charge for running the fund. Standard: **2% per annum on

committed capital during the ~5-year investment period, then 2% on invested capital (or NAV) during the harvest period** — the base shrinks as the portfolio is monetised, so the fee falls in later years.

  1. Carried interest (carry) — the GP's share of profits, standard 20%, and only after you have

been made whole to the hurdle.

  1. Preferred return (hurdle) — the minimum return you receive on committed capital before the GP earns

any carry. Standard: 8% IRR, compounded annually.

  1. GP catch-up — once the hurdle is paid, the GP takes 100% of the next slice of profits until its

cumulative share of total profits equals 20%; after that, the split moves to 80-20.

The catch-up is the term most first-time LPs skip. Its job is mechanical: without it, a fund that clears the hurdle by even a little would pay the GP almost nothing, because the GP only earns 20% after the hurdle. The catch-up gives the GP a fast lane back to its proportional 20% share, which is exactly why a 100% catch-up is GP-friendly and a 50% or no catch-up is LP-friendly.

<2>A worked fund: ₹500 Cr committed, 3x over 10 years

Take a ₹500 crore fund that returns ₹1,500 crore over ten years (3x gross, ~25% gross IRR), standard 2/20 economics with an 8% hurdle and a 100% catch-up:

StepWhat happensAmount (Cr)
1,LPs get their committed capital back,5002,LPs get the 8% preferred return (compounded over 10 years),~5793,Catch-up: GP takes 100% of the next slice until GP profit = 20% of total profit,~145 to GP4,Remaining profit split 80-20: ₹276 Cr x 80% to LPs, x 20% to GP,221 to LP, 55 to GP,Total to LPs (500 + 579 + 221),~1,300,Total GP carry (145 + 55),~200,Plus cumulative management fee over 10 years,~70-80

The GP's all-in economics (fee + carry) land around 5-6% of fund value on a success. Two things to take from that:

  • Your gross multiple and your net outcome are different animals. The fund "did 3x" — but a large chunk

of that gross profit is what the fee ladder kept. The net IRR to LPs here is around 10%, not the 25% gross figure the marketing would lead with.

  • An unsuccessful fund pays the GP only the management fee. For a ₹500 Cr fund that is the same ₹70-80

Cr over ten years with no carry. That is the alignment you are buying: the GP only does well if you do.

$$ C \;=\; \frac{\text{LP hurdle increment} \times \text{carry}}{1-\text{carry}} $$

For the worked fund the LP hurdle increment is ₹79 Cr (₹579 Cr preferred total minus ₹500 Cr capital), so the 100% catch-up is $79 \times 0.20 / 0.80 \approx ₹19.75$ Cr. The catch-up zone is always capped by the actual profit above the hurdle — a fund that clears the hurdle by only ₹5 Cr pays the GP at most ₹5 Cr in the catch-up zone. That cap, and every other step, is done for you in the AIF Waterfall calculator below.

<2>What actually moves in negotiation

The fee percentage (2%) and the carry percentage (20%) are anchored in market convention and barely move. What a sophisticated LP negotiates is:

LeverStandardWhere it movesWhy it matters
Hurdle,8% IRR,Up to 9-10% (debt / infra strategies),The single most-negotiated term. Raising the hurdle from 8 to 10% cut the GP's total carry by ~₹20-25 Cr on the worked fundCatch-up,100% (GP-friendly),50% or none (LP-friendly),Affects timing of the GP's carry in a successful fund, and barely matters in an unsuccessful oneFee base,Committed capital (investment period),Invested capital or NAV,Committed-capital fees charge you on money not yet deployed — the GP-friendly baseClawback,20-30% escrow holdback,Enforceable vs. paper-only,See below

The practical insight from practitioner guidance: first-time GPs often concede on the carry percentage (the wrong thing to concede) when the right move is to concede on the catch-up. The carry percentage is permanent for the fund's life; the catch-up change matters only in a successful fund.

<2>The clawback — the term that protects you when the fund misses

A fund can pay the GP carry early (interim distributions), then end its life below the hurdle. The clawback clause makes the GP return that excess carry. Two structural facts make it fragile in India:

  • A poorly drafted clawback can require the GP to return money **years after it has been distributed to the

GP's individual partners** — practically unenforceable.

  • The standard fix is an escrow holdback of 20-30% of every carry payment, released only when the fund

confirms at wind-up that the hurdle was met. Read the LPA for which version you got.

<2>The decision test for your ₹1 Cr ticket

You are the smallest investor in any of these structures, which means the fixed 2% fee is your largest relative drag. Before committing:

  1. Run the numbers at your size. A ₹1 Cr position in a fund with the worked economics: the 2% fee is

₹20,000/year regardless of your outcome — that is 20% of a reasonable ₹1L net annual profit before any carry. The fee-decay decision test applies.

  1. Ask for the waterfall summary page of the PPM, not the marketing deck. The four levers above are in

it; the marketing deck is not.

  1. Check the clawback and escrow language. If the clawback is paper-only with no holdback, value the GP's

carry as already spent, and price the fund lower.

  1. Use the AIF Waterfall calculator with the fund's actual terms — not the

2/20 defaults — to see your net multiple and net IRR, with and without the catch-up.
