Surcharge, Cess & the High-Income Regime: What ₹50L+ Actually Costs in FY 2026-27 (and the 25% Cap That Changes Everything Above ₹2Cr)

The high-income layer over the tax model: the three-tier computation (slab base tax → surcharge with marginal relief → 4% cess), the FY 2026-27 change that abolished the 37% surcharge in the new regime (capped at 25%), the 15% equity-surcharge cap, and when the old regime actually wins again at the very top.

DhanPedia · Author Chirag Singhal · Verified 2026-10-18

Below ₹50L the tax model is just slabs plus a 4% cess line — the 80C basket decision article covers that world, and its calculator deliberately excluded surcharge. Above ₹50L, two more layers switch on, and in FY 2026-27 one of them changed in a way most tax articles still get wrong: the Income-tax Act, 2025 (effective 1 April 2026) abolished the 37% top surcharge in the new regime and capped it at 25%. The old regime keeps 37% above ₹5Cr. That single asymmetry is what decides regime fights at the top of your capital ladder.

<2>The three tiers: base tax → surcharge → cess

Every bill above ₹50L is computed in exactly this order:

  1. Base tax — your slab tax (new or old regime) plus the separate-head tax on equity gains

(112A LTCG at 12.5% above the shared ₹1.25L pool; 111A STCG at 20%).

  1. Surcharge — a percentage of the base tax (not of income), in bands by total income.
  2. Marginal relief — at each surcharge boundary, the extra (tax + surcharge) on income above the

boundary cannot exceed the incremental income itself. This removes the "cliff effect": crossing ₹50L by ₹1,000 costs about ₹1,040 with relief, not roughly ₹1.1L without it.

  1. Health & education cess — a flat 4% on (base tax + surcharge after relief), in both

regimes, with no threshold.

$$ \text{Total liability} \;=\; \underbrace{T}_{\text{base tax}} \;+\; \underbrace{(S - MR)}_{\text{surcharge after marginal relief}} \;+\; 0.04\,(T + S - MR) $$

Total incomeNew regimeOld regime
Up to ₹50L,Nil,Nil₹50L – ₹1Cr,10%,10%₹1Cr – ₹2Cr,15%,15%₹2Cr – ₹5Cr,25%,25%Above ₹5Cr,25% (capped),37%

The two regimes are identical up to ₹5Cr. Above ₹5Cr the new regime stops at 25% while the old regime reaches 37% — a 12-percentage-point wedge on the surcharge layer alone, before you even compare the lower new-regime slabs. The 37% top rate of the 1961 Act was deliberately not carried into the new regime; the cap is a policy choice to keep very high earners in the default regime.

<2>The 15% equity-surcharge cap: the rule long-term equity investors need

Surcharge on tax arising from listed-equity gains — 111A short-term (20%) and 112A long-term (12.5%) — is capped at 15%, no matter how high your total income is. The slab portion of your base tax still attracts the full band rate. Consequence at, say, a ₹3Cr+ income:

Base-tax componentSurcharge applied
Equity LTCG/STCG tax,15% (capped)Everything else (salary, rent, interest, unlisted gains, business),Full band (25% / 37% by regime)

This is why a ₹10Cr earner whose income is mostly 112A LTCG pays far less effective surcharge than a ₹10Cr earner whose income is salary — and why the loss-harvesting and tax-free-stack articles keep pushing equity activity into the 112A bucket: the 12.5% rate and the 15% surcharge cap stack into the most tax-efficient long-term channel in the system.

The pattern to internalize: at every income tested, the new regime wins on total bill — but the margin is not constant. Between ₹50L and roughly ₹1–2Cr a heavy old-regime deduction stack (home-loan interest, 80D, 80E) can still flip the decision, as the regime-decision article shows. Above ~₹2Cr, where both regimes sit in the 25% band and the new regime's slabs are structurally lower, the new regime pulls away — and above ₹5Cr the 25%-vs-37% wedge makes the new regime the unambiguous choice for a high-earner without a very large deduction stack.

<2>Marginal relief, shown at the ₹50L boundary

Crossing ₹50L (self-employed, no standard deduction): the surcharge band turns from 0% to 10%. Without relief, the extra ₹1,000 of income would pull a 10% surcharge across the entire base tax (about ₹1.08L), so the marginal cost of that ₹1,000 would be ₹10,800+. With marginal relief the extra liability is capped at the incremental income — the verified figure is ₹1,040 (₹1,000 plus a sliver of slab-rate tax), not the cliff. The same cap operates at ₹1Cr, ₹2Cr and ₹5Cr. In practice this means: income that lands just above a boundary is nearly as cheap as income at the boundary — and it removes most of the temptation to "save for next year" an income component that is already going to happen (you usually cannot choose the timing of salary).
