# Gold, Silver & Commodities: Routes, Currency, Hedging & Limits

Physical vs ETF vs fund vs SGB, hallmarking and spreads, silver routes, commodity futures, currency effects, and what gold actually does — and doesn't — hedge.

Gold is the only asset in a typical Indian portfolio that is at once a store of value, a currency hedge, a wedding tradition, a jewellery heirloom, and a commodity with a global futures market. Those identities do not always agree with each other, and the disagreements are where most investor losses happen: buying at the wedding season, paying jewellery making charges as if they were price, treating a gold ETF like a savings account, or selling "investment gold" in the same shops that bought it from you. This article maps every route Indian investors actually have — physical, ETF, funds, SGB (secondary market only), digital, silver, and futures — and then gets blunt about what gold hedges, what it does not, and why commodity futures are where small accounts go to die.

THE HEDGING QUESTION FIRST: WHAT GOLD DOES, AND DOESN'T
=======================================================

Before you compare routes, fix what the asset is for, because every route decision below follows from it.

What gold reliably does:
• Hedges currency devaluation. India imports most of its gold, so domestic prices are a function of international price plus the rupee. When the rupee weakens, domestic gold rises even if the world price is flat. For an Indian saver, that property is half the point: gold in your safe is a partial reserve against INR depreciation that no INR asset can give you.
• Acts as a fear asset. In sharp equity drawdowns, gold frequently rises or at least holds, because it is the canonical non-correlated reserve. Its long-run correlation with Indian equities has been low but not zero, and in stress episodes it can move with everything; treat "uncorrelated" as a description of average conditions, not a guarantee for the one crash you actually live through.
• Partially, imperfectly hedges inflation. Gold has preserved purchasing power over very long spans, which is the original argument. But "inflation hedge" over-debates the answer: in the short to medium run gold's returns are driven by real interest rates (gold pays no yield, so rising real yields hurt it) and by central-bank demand more than by the consumer price index. Gold can lag inflation for years in a row. It is a devaluation hedge, not a CPI hedge.

What gold does not do:
• It has negative carry. Gold pays no dividend, no interest, no cash flow. Your return is entirely price change, so every year you hold, you underperform a risk-free asset by the risk-free rate unless price rises by more than it. In years when real yields fall (the modern gold bull market), that cost is offset and more; when real yields rise, it is not.
• Rising real yields hurt it. The dominant economic driver of gold is the gap between gold's zero carry and the bond market's yield. A world where 10-year real yields climb is, all else equal, a headwind for gold.
• Long bull runs carry opportunity cost. Gold did not compound the way equities did through the great 2010s equity expansion; the "safe haven" is not the "return engine." This is why the sensible role for gold is a 5–10% portfolio allocation, not a core holding: enough to matter in the decade when everything else falls, not so much that it quietly drags a 30-year return.
• It is not a substitute for insurance or equity allocation decisions. A high gold allocation does not make a plan safer; it just adds one more asset with its own cycle to a plan that still needs the other pieces in place.

ROUTE BY ROUTE
==============

PHYSICAL: JEWELLERY AND BULLION
===============================

The oldest route, and the one where the spread quietly eats your wealth.

Hallmarking is now the first thing to check. The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) hallmarks jewellery at 916 (22k, 375/375 parts gold by weight) and bullion at 999/999.9 (24k). An un-hallmarked piece is a price negotiation, not a purity guarantee — the BIS hallmarked mark is what separates verifiable purity from seller's word, and it is the single most important purchase discipline for physical gold.

Jewellery. The making charge (typically a few hundred to a few thousand rupees per gram depending on the design and city, a pattern not a universal number) is manufacturing margin you pay on the way in and cannot recover on the way out. The resale discount against prevailing 916 prices is real and consistent, with buy-sell spreads of 5–8% of the purchase price being a typical, pattern-level figure for jewellery — verify with the specific jeweller you deal with, because the spread is set locally, not centrally. The honest conclusion: jewellery is a wearing asset that coincidentally stores value; it is not an investment route. Buy it for the occasion, track its value with humility, and do not plan a retirement on the wedding gold.

Bullion (coins and bars). Lower making-charge problem than jewellery (coin premiums exist, but there are no "making charges"), and you can compare the premium over the prevailing 999 price across jewellers. The trade-off: security. A home vault is not free.

Spreads, the whole picture. Every physical route has a spread: buy at the jeweller's price (spot + premium + making), sell at spot − discount. For a 5–8% two-way spread on Rs. 1Cr of gold, that is Rs. 50,000–Rs. 80,000 that exists only as a transaction cost. It is not "price going up"; it is the price of holding a physical route.

SGBS — DISCONTINUED AS A NEW ISSUE, OWNABLE ONLY VIA SECONDARY MARKET
=====================================================================

Sovereign Gold Bonds were the cleanest gold route India ever offered: the government backed the principal and paid interest, so you could earn a cash return on paper gold, plus the appreciation itself. That combination has a tax consequence worth remembering for anyone holding SGBs bought earlier: on maturity or redemption through the primary/secondary channel, the gains on SGBs are treated as tax-exempt capital gains on redemption (verify current treatment, and note the interest is fully taxable at your slab rate, unlike the redemption itself).

The status you must know: the SGB scheme stopped issuing new tranches from FY 2024-25, with the last tranche issued in February 2024 (verified 2026-08-06, src-sgb-discontinued). The source registry itself flags the limitation: confirm the current-year status against the latest Union Budget / Ministry of Finance note before republishing — a re-launch is a budget-announced thing, not a standing schedule.

What this means practically. Existing SGBs are a real and still-traded asset, listed on exchanges; the secondary market has been active enough to buy with a modest premium/discount to the underlying gold value. The tax structure that made SGBs attractive is unchanged for these bonds, so the secondary market is still a legitimate route for gold exposure with interest, just one you enter as a bond, not an allocation plan. New-issuance SGBs: do not plan around them; treat their return as a budget-dependent option. The original feature set, for reference (8-year maturity, 2.5% p.a. interest, tax-exempt on maturity) is captured in src-indiagold; that document predates the discontinuation, so read it as "how it worked" and cross-check against src-sgb-discontinued for "what is on offer now."

GOLD ETFS
=========

An exchange-traded fund that holds either physical gold (via the issuer's depository arrangement) or SGBs as the underlying — the structure differs by fund and the fund's prospectus states which. You buy on NSE/BSE in rupee, in demat account, in fractional units the way you buy any listed ETF.

The spread problem disappears; the tax question does not. No making charge, no 5–8% spread — the cost is a small premium/discount to the NAV plus the ETF's expense ratio. The tax treatment of gains on gold ETFs has been a subject of change: gains on gold ETFs (and the underlying SGBs they hold) have been treated under equity-tax rules for many funds — long-term capital gains treatment for holdings beyond a 12-month hold period — with the exact current position depending on the specific fund, its underlying (SGB vs physical), and current tax law. Mark as: verify current treatment before relying on the "equity treatment" line; the general pattern is that most gold ETFs are taxed like equity, but read the specific fund's fact-sheet language and current law, not this article's summary, for your case.

The real edge of the ETF route: it is the only large-scale route that is liquid daily, demat-held, and priced continuously against spot, which makes it the natural vehicle for the 5–10% allocation. The other routes are warehouses for the allocation.

GOLD MUTUAL FUNDS
=================

Open-end funds that hold gold ETFs or SGBs as the underlying. They are the non-exchange version of the ETF route — same exposure, different access (lump-sum/SIP through any mutual-fund platform, no demat needed). For a saver who wants a disciplined monthly allocation into gold (a classic SIP pattern), the gold MF is the cleanest tool: no minimum lot size, no dealing in an exchange, and the SIP cadence itself is the behaviour management that most one-shot gold purchases lack.

The tax treatment follows the underlying — check the fund's fact-sheet, and as with ETFs, verify current classification.

DIGITAL GOLD
============

Gold bought in gram-fractions through an app or the jeweller's own platform. The honest framing: digital gold is a claim on the jeweller's inventory, not a sovereign claim, not a government-guaranteed claim. If the jeweller defaults, your "gold" is a receivable. A digital-gold wallet is also not a registered investment — you are buying metal through a merchant, not a security. The route has a legitimate niche (small, liquid, no making charge), and the tax treatment at sale has had its own set of changes, so verify the current treatment. The one thing it should not be is the main store of your gold allocation, for the simple reason that the counterparty is a jeweller, not a regulator.

SILVER
======

Silver is a smaller, more industrial, more volatile commodity. The routes mirror gold: silver ETFs (the liquid route), silver mutual funds, physical silver (with the same hallmarking and spread problems, plus the fact that silver bullion is a thinner market than gold bullion), and futures (which, for the reasons in the next section, are off the table for most retail). Silver's price action is dominated by industrial demand (electronics, solar), which gives it a different cycle and a generally higher volatility than gold. For an Indian portfolio, if gold is the currency/fear hedge, silver is a higher-beta industrial commodity with a precious-metal skin — an 0–2% allocation for the investor who explicitly wants that skin, not a substitute for gold's role.

COMMODITY FUTURES — THE DANGEROUS ROUTE
=======================================

India has commodity futures on MCX and NSE for a set of commodities including gold, silver, crude, and a basket of agricultural commodities. The structure is: you take a directional position, with leverage (margin, not notional), on a contract that expires, and in most contracts the settlement is cash, not physical delivery.

Why this combination is a dangerous one for retail:
• Leverage, both directions. You post 10–15% of the notional as margin (a pattern, verify the current exchange margin) to control a much larger position. When the market is with you, that's a multiplier on returns; when it's against you, it's a multiplier on losses, fast.
• Expiries force behaviour. The contract dies at a scheduled date. To stay in the market, you must roll — sell the old contract and open a new one — incurring a spread cost every cycle. In a sideways market, roll costs can grind down a position that is, on paper, "not losing."
• Cash settlement means no physical gold. A retail trader who "buys gold futures" on MCX is not taking delivery of gold; the contract settles in cash, and your risk is the price, not the metal.
• Commodity cycles are not equity cycles. A gold bull that took three years can give it back in three months, with the margin structure amplifying both the gain and the loss in that window.
• Most retail does not have an edge. The other side of the MCX counter is a dealer with a hedging book. Your "view on gold" is not the same thing as a price forecast the market has already partly priced.

The honest framing: futures are where a portfolio's hedge gets implemented by a fund manager with a real hedging mandate. They are not where a Rs. 25L retail account builds wealth. The allocation answer for retail is the ETF or MF route, not the futures route.

CURRENCY: THE ONE VARIABLE THAT DOES NOT GET ENOUGH ATTENTION
=============================================================

Domestic gold price (INR per gram) = international price (USD per ounce) × USD/INR rate ÷ conversion constants + import duties.

A weak rupee raises domestic gold even if the world price is flat. A strong rupee drops domestic gold even if the world price rises. This is the mechanism that makes gold an INR-hedge, and it is also the mechanism by which an Indian gold holder's "return" is decoupled from the "gold return" the news channel is talking about. The two returns can move in opposite directions in a given quarter: the world price up 5%, the rupee up 3%, and domestic gold is down for Indian holders.

The same mechanism works in reverse for silver. For futures, it compounds: currency risk plus leverage plus expiry means the "gold" position is four different risks in one, and the typical retail trader is under-hedged on three of them.

SIZING: THE 5–10% GUIDANCE AND WHY IT IS THE NUMBER
===================================================

The 5–10% allocation to gold in a diversified Indian portfolio is not a return target; it is a risk-budget statement. The logic:
• Below 5%, the hedge is too small to matter when the currency devalues or the fear spike hits.
• Above 10%, the negative carry starts to visibly drag a 30-year return, and the allocation starts to feel like a gold position rather than a gold hedge.
• Within 5–10%, the allocation behaves as insurance against the tail that does not show up in the annual return table — the rupee crisis, the sovereign de-rating, the fear spike. The 5–10% figure is a pattern consistent with the "tail hedge" role; it is not a sourced constant, and it should be revisited as the portfolio's equity weight and risk budget change.

For the silver add-on, 0–2% of the total portfolio, or 0–30% of the gold sleeve if you want to keep the precious-metal sleeve concentrated.

THE HONEST LIMITS OF "GOLD AS A HEDGE"
======================================

Three limits that should keep a 5–10% allocation from becoming a 40% "safe" allocation:
• The hedge is to currency and fear, not to the equity portfolio's own drawdown. In a 2020-style crash, gold did hold up. In a 2022-style real-yields-shock, gold fell with risk assets for a period. The hedge is to a category of shock, not to all shocks.
• Real-yield rising is the single most consistent headwind. You cannot tell from the price chart alone whether gold is "rising on strength" or "rising on a lagging real-yield cycle." The real-yield channel is the one that breaks most naive "gold is always up" narratives.
• The opportunity cost is real and long. A 30-year hold of gold vs a 30-year hold of a diversified equity portfolio is a different asset, with different return, different risk, and different carry. The 5–10% allocation is the honest answer that respects both the hedge value and the cost.
