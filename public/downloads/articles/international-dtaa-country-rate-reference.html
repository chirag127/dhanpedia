DTAA Country-Rate Reference: Which Treaty Rate Applies to Your India-Source Income (FY 2026-27)

The treaty-rate reference the NRI article deliberately left out: India's 90+ DTAA corridors side-by-side (dividends / interest / royalties / FTS) against the domestic Act rate, the two relief methods (exemption vs credit), how the lower rate wins (Sec 90 / 90A / 159(4)), the TRC + Form 41 / 10F + Form 67 claim process, and why most treaties do NOT reduce capital gains.

DhanPedia · Author Chirag Singhal · Verified 2026-10-18

The NRI tax article covered how an NRI claims DTAA relief — the Tax Residency Certificate, Form 41 (formerly 10F), and Form 67. But it deliberately left out the part everyone actually looks up at the moment of a payment: what is the treaty rate for my country, on this type of income? This article is that reference. It puts India's 90-plus DTAA corridors side-by-side against the domestic Act rate, explains the two relief methods, and walks the claim end-to-end. Figures marked (verified 2026-10-18) come from the cited sources; treaty rates are the typical corridor rate, and the exact figure depends on the income type and any holding condition — confirm the treaty text before you act.

<2>The two relief methods (and why most corridors are "credit")

A DTAA stops the same income being fully taxed twice. It does that in one of two ways, and the method is decided by which country has the first right to tax the income in that treaty:

MethodHow it worksWhere India is the source countryExamples
Exemption,Income is taxed only in one country; the other exempts it,Home country exempts, or India exempts,UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait (no / low home-side income tax)Credit,Income is taxed in both; the residence country credits the tax already paid in the source country,India withholds; home country credits it,USA, UK, Germany, Singapore, Australia, Canada

For an NRI with India-source income, the practical effect is the same: you want India to charge the treaty (lower) rate, not the domestic Act rate, and your home country to either exempt or credit what India took. The domestic fallback is what you pay if you never claim the treaty — that is the number this article's corridor table compares against. [src-dtaa-country-rates-2026]

<2>How the lower rate wins (Sec 90 / 90A / 159(4))
  • Section 90 (DTAA countries) and Section 90A (exemption-method countries) let the NRI be taxed

at the treaty rate when the treaty is more beneficial than the Act.

  • Section 159(4) is the switch: the tax is computed under the Act, but where the treaty

provides a lower rate, the treaty rate applies — provided the non-resident supplies the proof.

  • The proof is the Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) from your home country plus Form 41 (the

NRI self-declaration that replaces the older Form 10F). Hand both to the payer before it deducts; the payer then withholds at the lower treaty rate.

  • If the treaty also credits the home country, you file Form 67 with your Indian ITR to claim

the foreign tax credit (now Section 91 under the Income-tax Act 2025) for tax paid abroad. The credit is capped at the lesser of the India-side tax paid and the home-country tax on the same income — you cannot come out ahead of the higher of the two. [src-nri-tax-tds-dtaa-2026]

[src-dtaa-country-rates-2026]

The capital-gains caveat that ruins the easy assumption: most DTAAs do not let you reduce the tax on the sale of shares or property below India's rate. India keeps its taxing right on gains from Indian shares (111A / 112A) and immovable property (12.5% no-indexation). The treaty relief that matters to an individual NRI is on dividends, interest, royalties and fees — not on realising your gains. (A few older treaties had special share-gain protections for pre-2017 holdings; treat gains as India-taxed unless the treaty text says otherwise.) [src-nri-tax-tds-dtaa-2026]

Alt text: Flow for India-source income of an NRI: the payer withholds at the domestic Act rate unless you hand over the TRC + Form 41/10F (then the lower treaty rate); you then file ITR-2 to reclaim over-withholding; the home country either exempts (exemption-method, e.g. UAE) or credits the India tax (Form 67 / section 91), so the net is at most the higher of the two countries' taxes.

<2>Surcharge and cess still ride on top

The treaty rate caps the base withholding; it does not delete surcharge or the 4% Health & Education cess, both of which stack onto the (treaty) tax before it is remitted. That is why the all-in effective rate is higher than the headline treaty rate: the surcharge / cess article carries the FY 2026-27 surcharge schedule, and the calculator shows both the pre-cess saving and the all-in figure for your corridor. [src-it-slab-fy2026-27]

<2>How to claim (the exact sequence, in order)
  1. Get the Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) from your home country's tax authority. It is valid

for the financial year it is issued for — renew it yearly.

  1. File Form 41 (the NRI declaration; the renamed Form 10F) on the Indian e-filing portal,

and keep the acknowledgement. This is what tells the payer you are a non-resident entitled to the treaty.

  1. Hand PAN + TRC + Form 41 acknowledgement to the payer / bank before the payment date. The

payer withholds at the lower treaty rate only when this is in place; missing the payment date means it withholds at the domestic rate and you chase the refund.

  1. File your Indian ITR (ITR-2, due 31 Jul) to reclaim any over-withheld TDS — the ITR is

the only route to the refund; TDS already deducted does not disappear by itself.

  1. Claim the foreign tax credit — Form 67, credit under Section 91 (exemption-method

corridors have nothing to credit; credit-method countries cap the credit at the lesser of the India-side tax and the home-side tax on the same income).

Two NRI-only traps, repeated here from the NRI article: NRIs cannot use Form 15G / 15H (the nil-TDS declarations are resident-only), and the 87A rebate does not apply to an NRI — an NRI is taxed on the whole India-source income with no ₹12L tax-free threshold.

<2>When the treaty does NOT help (the honest limits)
  • Capital gains on Indian shares / property — most treaties leave India's rate intact.
  • A payer who withholds before you send the TRC + Form 41 — you pay the domestic rate and must

reclaim via the ITR; the money is locked meanwhile.

  • A home country whose credit is lower than India's tax — the net is still bounded by the

higher country; you cannot use the treaty to manufacture a below-both-countries result.

  • GAAR / beneficial-ownership tests — if the structure is seen as the principal purpose of

avoiding tax, the treaty benefit can be denied (Principal Purpose Test on newer MLI treaties).
