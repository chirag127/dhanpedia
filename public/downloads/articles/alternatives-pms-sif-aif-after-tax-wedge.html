The After-Tax Wedge: Why Equal Gross Returns Leave You Different Money (Index vs PMS vs SIF vs AIF)

The 'what actually lands in your pocket' layer over the capital ladder: an index fund, a PMS, a SIF, a Category I/II AIF and a Category III AIF can all quote the same gross return - but the net CAGR that compounds in your hands differs because of where the tax lands and the fee stack. A worked 10-year comparison on 14% gross.

DhanPedia · Author Chirag Singhal · Verified 2026-10-18

You now have the three layers of the professional rungs: the selection & fee-decay test, the category decision, and the 2/20 waterfall. This article is the layer most investors skip, and the one that quietly decides the answer to "which of these is actually better for me?": what the return is after tax and after fees, on the same gross number.

<2>The single question to ask any product

Every product on the ladder will quote you a gross return: a PMS and a SIF show net-of-fee portfolios, an AIF PPM shows a gross IRR, an index fund shows the index. Those are not comparable until you answer one question:

If I put the same ₹10L into each, at the same gross return, over the same years — which one leaves me the most money in my own bank account, and at what annualized rate does that grow?

The gap between a product's gross CAGR and its after-tax, after-fee CAGR is the wedge. The wedge is wider the more the tax lands at a high rate on your gain, and the more fees drag the compounding.

Read that table the right way: at 14% gross, an index fund is the most tax-efficient home for that return. A PMS earns its fees by outperforming the index — but you only benefit net if its alpha exceeds its fee-and-tax wedge over a plain index, which is the exact test in the selection guide. The AIFs are not "better returns" at equal gross; they are different-asset-class returns (unlisted, pre-IPO, private credit) that a listed fund cannot reach — the wedge is the price of that access.

A Cat III is a satellite, taxed net of the fund's ~42.7%.

The decision rule: a higher-fee, higher-tax product is only worth it if its gross edge over the cheaper alternative × (1 − wedge) is still positive. That multiplication is why a PMS that "beats the index by 300 bps gross" can lose to the index after its own fee-and-tax wedge — and why an unlisted AIF that returns 14% gross can hand you less per rupee than a 12% index.

<2>Three traps that make the wedge look smaller than it is
  1. The 194LBB TDS cash-flow trap. A Cat I/II AIF credits you income (and 10% TDS) before you are

paid out. You can owe advance tax on money that never hit your bank account — the advance-tax clocks still run on the credit. The calculator shows the exit tax; the TDS is a cash-flow problem on top of it.

  1. The shared ₹1.25L pool resets yearly but a 10-year exit uses one. The calculator zeroes the pool

for a multi-year single exit because the pool is an annual equity-LTCG bucket; a huge exit in one year is taxed above that one year's ₹1.25L, not a 10-year accumulated pool.

  1. Cat III "net of tax" can hide the surcharge. The ~42.7% is with surcharge at the fund level; a

specific trust deed and tax note can change it. Read the fund tax note, as the AIF taxation article says.
