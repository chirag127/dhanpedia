# Your 10 Lakh Wealth Plan: Allocation, SIP Scaling & the Road to PMS/AIF

A concrete, risk-graded plan for a ₹10L starter portfolio with a growth path into higher-return, higher-capital avenues (PMS, AIF).

A Rs. 10 lakh portfolio is the stage where most Indian investors stop planning and start doing: the emergency fund is built, insurance is in place, and a first meaningful chunk of investable capital is ready. But "I have Rs. 10,00,000 — now what?" has no single answer. It depends on your risk grade, your horizon, your tax regime, and whether the plan has a growth path that does not strand you in a SIP-only mindset forever.

This article gives you a concrete, risk-graded plan for a Rs. 10,00,000 starter portfolio: exact allocation amounts for three risk grades, the SIP scaling math with the formula shown, a growth path that leads into PMS and AIFs when capital supports them, and honest about when those higher-capital avenues are the wrong tool. Everything below is educational and illustrative — not individualized advice. Numbers labelled "assumption" or "illustrative" are planning inputs you should stress-test with your own income and risk tolerance; numbers anchored to regulation or current policy are marked with their verification date.

THREE RISK GRADES: PICK ONE, KNOW WHY
=====================================

You cannot allocate Rs. 10 lakh across five sleeves without first deciding how much pain you can actually sit through. We define three grades. The labels are about your behaviour in a −30% equity year, not about the markets.

Text version of the diagram: one entry node (your Rs. 10 lakh) branches into three grades; each grade expands into its sleeve split.

Sleeve: Index equity (Nifty 50 + Nifty 500 ETFs/funds; maximum adds small & mid flexi),35% → ₹3.5 lakh,55% → ₹5.5 lakh,70% → ₹7.0 lakh  |  Conservative: PPF + ELSS (longer-horizon tax-efficient sleeve),30% → ₹3.0 lakh,20% → ₹2.0 lakh,12% → ₹1.2 lakh  |  Aggressive: Gold (gold ETFs; SGBs are no longer the route — see update log),10% → ₹1.0 lakh,10% → ₹1.0 lakh,8% → ₹0.8 lakh  |  Maximum: Bonds / SCSS when eligible,15% → ₹1.5 lakh,8% → ₹0.8 lakh,6% → ₹0.6 lakh

What each sleeve is doing. The index sleeve is the compounder: low-cost, Nifty 50 plus Nifty 500 exposure, bought partly as a lump sum and partly as a monthly SIP so that market timing is mostly removed. The PPF + ELSS sleeve is the tax-advantaged, long-tenor middle: PPF runs 15 years at ~7.1–7.2% p.a. (last verified: 2026-08-06) with tax-exempt interest, and ELSS gives you a three-year equity lock-in (per the SEBI investor-education guide to ELSS, src-sebi-elss). The gold sleeve uses ETFs rather than Sovereign Gold Bonds, because SGBs stopped accepting new tranches from FY 2024-25 (last tranche February 2024; status re-check against the latest Budget note — src-sgb-discontinued). The bonds/SCSS sleeve earns the repo-linked fixed-income anchor — with policy repo near 5.25% through late 2025 (last verified: 2026-08-06, src-rbi-repo); SCSS at 8.2% p.a. for FY 2025-26 (last verified: 2026-08-06) applies only to seniors (60+), so for a 20s–40s reader that sleeve is short-duration bond funds and a PPF-heavy tilt instead, and the SCSS rate becomes relevant to you much later or to your parents' planning. The P2P sleeve is the satellite: a small, capped slice in RBI-regulated peer-to-peer lending for a yield pickup over bonds, sized so a bad vintage cannot hurt. We deliberately keep it at 4–7% of portfolio in every grade; the full due-diligence framework lives in p2p-lending-complete.

Choosing your grade. Conservative suits anyone with near-term goal overlap, a young family without dual income, or genuine inability to watch a 30% drawdown. Aggressive is the default for a stable-income reader in their 20s–30s with 15+ years to the goal. Maximum is for 30s–40s readers with high and growing income, no dependents at risk, and a written statement that they will add (not panic-sell) in a crash. Write the choice down. The grade decides the numbers in the table; the table decides the behaviour.

SIP SCALING: THE MATH THAT MOVES THE NEEDLE
===========================================

A Rs. 10 lakh lump is a good start, but the plan's engine is the monthly SIP. The standard formula for the future value of a monthly SIP (ordinary annuity, monthly compounding of the annual CAGR) is:

$$FV \;=\; P \times \frac{(1+i)^{n}-1}{i}, \qquad i=\frac{CAGR}{12},\quad n=\text{number of months}$$

Worked example, stated precisely so you can reproduce it: P = Rs. 15,000/month, assumed CAGR = 12%, horizon 15 years = 180 months, so i = 1%.
1. Monthly growth factor: $(1+i) = 1.01$
2. $(1.01)^{180} = e^{180 \times \ln 1.01} = e^{180 \times 0.00995} = e^{1.7911} \approx 5.996$
3. Annuity factor: $\frac{5.996 - 1}{0.01} \approx 499.6$
4. $FV = 15{,}000 \times 499.6 \approx \text{Rs. }74{,}94{,}000$

So Rs. 15,000 a month at a 12% assumed CAGR for 15 years yields ≈ Rs. 74.9 lakh, against Rs. 27.0 lakh actually invested (15,000 × 180) — the gain is roughly Rs. 47.9 lakh, and nearly two-thirds of the final corpus is compounding, not contributions. (Be wary of figures like Rs. 44.7 lakh floating around for this exact setup: that number comes from a shorter horizon or a lower assumed CAGR. Show the formula; check the exponent.)

Two refinements, honestly labelled:
• Annuity-due variant. SIPs are paid at the start of the month, so each instalment earns one extra month: $FV_{due} = FV_{ordinary} \times (1+i) \approx 74.9 \times 1.01 \approx Rs. 75.7$ lakh. The difference is small; the habit of stating which convention you used is not optional.
• Step-ups change everything. If your income grows and you raise the SIP 10% every year (a growing annuity: year-1 SIP Rs. 1.8 lakh, year-2 Rs. 1.98 lakh, … at the same 12% planning CAGR), the 15-year SIP corpus alone rises to ≈ Rs. 1.23 crore (illustrative, same assumptions; with the Rs. 10 lakh lump included, the total is ≈ Rs. 1.78 crore). Step-ups are the single largest behavioural lever in the whole plan — larger than any allocation tweak. The mechanism: a higher contribution in later years compounds for fewer years, but the sheer size of the contribution dominates; the two effects together turn a Rs. 74.9 lakh outcome into Rs. 1.23 crore from the SIP stream alone.

The CAGR you will quote anywhere else in this plan is defined as:

$$CAGR = \left(\frac{V_{end}}{V_{begin}}\right)^{1/t} - 1$$

where t is in years. Keep the pair: the formula for building (SIP FV) and the formula for checking (CAGR).

GROWTH PATH: FROM RS. 10 LAKH TO PMS/AIF TERRITORY
==================================================

SIPs do not age gracefully. Below roughly Rs. 50 lakh, index funds are simply the right vehicle. Beyond that, the menu opens — but only if you plan the transition instead of stumbling into it.

The milestone math, at the same 12% planning CAGR: Rs. 10 lakh lump + Rs. 15,000 monthly SIP reaches ≈ Rs. 48.8 lakh by year 8 (lump: 10 × 1.12⁸ ≈ Rs. 24.8 lakh; SIP: 15,000 × ((1.01)⁹⁶ − 1)/0.01 ≈ Rs. 24.0 lakh) and crosses Rs. 50 lakh around year 8–8.5. Illustrative, not a forecast.

  S["₹10L lump + ₹15k/mo SIP\n@12% planning CAGR"] --> M1["~₹50L corpus\n≈ year 8-8.5"]
  M1 --> M0["SIF sleeve available\n(SEBI 2026, ₹10L aggregate min)"]
  M1 --> M2["PMS-eligible\n(₹50 lakh regulatory minimum)"]
  M2 --> M3["AIF Category II / SIPP\ncapital scales further"]

Text version: the corpus path flows from the starter plan, through the Rs. 50 lakh PMS milestone, to AIF Category II territory as capital grows.
• SIF: the new bridge rung at your current corpus. SEBI’s 2026 Mutual Fund Regulations added the Specialized Investment Fund category (effective 1 April 2026) with a Rs. 10 lakh aggregate minimum per PAN within a single AMC’s SIF offerings. That is exactly your starting corpus, so for the first time a Rs. 10L investor can own a "sophisticated" strategy tier (dynamic allocation, sector rotation, capped long-short) inside a regulated pooled vehicle taxed like a mutual fund — without jumping straight to the Rs. 50L PMS gate. Rule of thumb: keep the SIF sleeve to roughly 15–25% of total corpus and only on top of a solid index/debt core. Full treatment: SIF: The Rs. 10 Lakh Tier (../alternatives/alternatives-sif-specialized-funds.mdx).

• PMS entry at Rs. 50 lakh. Portfolio Management Schemes have a Rs. 50 lakh minimum; above that the 1–2% management-fee structure becomes efficient only if the manager genuinely adds value against an index benchmark. The full anatomy — AIF Categories I/II/III, SEBI minimums, fee and lock-in comparisons, when PMS beats a fund house — is in alternatives-pms-aif-private. For this plan, treat Rs. 50 lakh as the first gate, not the last: a realistic 15-year outcome at these assumptions is a ≈ Rs. 1.3 crore corpus (Rs. 54.7 lakh from the lump: 10 × 1.12¹⁵ ≈ Rs. 54.7 lakh, plus the Rs. 74.9 lakh SIP corpus).
• AIF Category II for private/smids. Category II AIFs (typically long-only, no leverage beyond caps) are the natural home for private-market and small/mid-cap concentrated books once you have enough capital that a Rs. 50 lakh PMS no longer dwarfs your investable surplus. The rule of thumb: move a sleeve, never the whole plan, and only after the PMS stage.

WHEN NOT TO USE PMS/AIF
=======================

Higher capital access is not automatically better. Do not move into PMS/AIF when:
• Your horizon for that money is under 5 years. PMS lock-ins, AIF commitments and private-market illiquidity all assume you can wait through a full equity cycle.
• You need liquidity. A Rs. 50 lakh PMS position is not something you unwind in a week for a goal that moved up by two years.
• Your total investable capital is near the minimum. A 1.5% fee on a Rs. 50 lakh book that is benchmark-indexable is pure drag; index funds at ~0.1–0.5% expense ratios win on arithmetic alone.
• You have not written an Investment Policy Statement. PMS/AIF decisions without a written benchmark and sizing rule become concentration decisions in disguise.
• The alternative is boring but correct. If an index + gold + debt mix at your risk grade beats every available PMS on a risk-adjusted basis, stay in it.

TAX DRAG: HOW SLABS, 87A AND LOCK-INS SHAPE THE PLAN
====================================================

Under the new regime for FY 2025-26 (src-it-slab-fy2025-26), the individual slabs are:

Slab (new regime, FY 2025-26): Nil to ₹4,00,000,Nil  |  Rate: ₹4,00,001 – ₹8,00,000,5%

With the Rs. 75,000 standard deduction and the Rs. 60,000 Section 87A rebate, a salaried individual with taxable income up to Rs. 12,00,000 (i.e., gross salary up to Rs. 12.75 lakh) pays zero income tax. This is the quiet superpower of the new regime: most readers of a Rs. 10 lakh plan will live in the zero-tax zone for years, which means tax drag while accumulating is small. The drag concentrates at exits: long-term equity capital gains at 12.5% plus 4% cess (effective ~13%) on gains — last verified: 2026-08-06 — and short-term gains at 20%. Two worked intuitions:
• Realising Rs. 3 lakh of LCG costs you Rs. 37,500 tax plus Rs. 1,500 cess — Rs. 39,000, an effective 13% haircut on that slice.
• Dividends and bond interest are taxed at slab rates; for a reader inside 87A that tax is 0%, which is precisely why PPF's tax-exempt interest (~7.1–7.2%, last verified: 2026-08-06) is a real advantage in this plan and not just a marketing one.

Regime nuance, stated plainly: the Rs. 1.5 lakh 80C deduction (PPF, ELSS, etc.) and the extra Rs. 50,000 80CCD(1B) for NPS exist only under the old regime (80C limit: src-it-80c-150k). If you stay on the new regime, the PPF + ELSS sleeve in the tables above is valued for capital protection, tenor and tax-free compounding, not for the deduction. If your income puts you in a high slab where old-regime deductions pay more than 87A's rebate, the regime choice is a genuine arbitrage question — run it against the FY 2025-26 slabs above before you sign anything. (Full treatment in tax-india-investor-complete.)

PICK THE REGIME BY ARITHMETIC, NOT HABIT
========================================

The new regime is the default for FY 2026-27, and for most people at this income level it is the lower-tax choice even with zero deductions, because the slab structure was reset so that income up to Rs. 12 lakh is effectively tax-free (Rs. 12.75 lakh gross for a salaried person after the Rs. 75,000 standard deduction). The old regime only wins when your Chapter VI-A stack (80C, 80D, 24(b) home-loan interest, 80CCD-1B NPS, HRA) is large enough that its deductions outweigh the new regime’s flatter slabs — usually meaning a big home-loan or heavy insurance/NPS outlay, often somewhere above Rs. 20–25L of income. Work the number for your own facts both ways (the calculators page has the slab math) and commit to the lower-tax regime each year; it is a reversible, annual decision. Capital-gains and interest tax are outside this regime choice and are governed by the slab/CAPG rules in the taxation article.

KEY FORMULAS
============

$$FV = P\,\frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i} \qquad\text{(SIP future value; } i = \tfrac{CAGR}{12}\text{, } n \text{ in months)}$$

$$CAGR = \left(\frac{V_{end}}{V_{begin}}\right)^{1/t} - 1 \qquad\text{(t in years)}$$
