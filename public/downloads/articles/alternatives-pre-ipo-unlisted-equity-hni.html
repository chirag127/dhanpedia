Pre-IPO & Unlisted Equity: The Rung Between SIF and AIF (Lock-in, the Reg-17 Trap & the Tax Split Most Buyers Miss)

The direct private-market rung: how to lawfully buy pre-IPO/unlisted shares, why the ICDR Reg 17 six-month post-IPO lock-in means you cannot sell into the listing pop, why SEBI does not recognise the off-market platforms, where the supply comes from, and the 24-month vs 12-month / 112 vs 112A tax split that decides what you actually keep.

DhanPedia · Author Chirag Singhal · Verified 2026-10-18

Your capital ladder so far: a SIF at ₹10L, a PMS at ₹50L, an AIF at ₹1Cr. This article is the rung most investors try to skip directly — buying pre-IPO / unlisted equity in your own demat, off-market, before the IPO — and the three structural facts that make it both the most misunderstood and the most expensive to get wrong.

<2>The three facts that define this market

1. You cannot sell into the listing pop (ICDR Regulation 17). Pre-IPO shares held by anyone other than the promoters are locked in for six months from the date of IPO allotment, under Regulation 17 of the SEBI ICDR Regulations. That single rule means: the listing-day price is not a price you can sell into. You carry six months of post-listing volatility with no ability to exit — recent listings have moved 30-40% inside that window, in both directions. The correct question is therefore not "what will it pop to on listing day?" but "at what price does it trade six months after listing, and is my entry below that?"

A worked case from 2026 makes the point concrete: unlisted NSE buyers paid roughly ₹1,950-2,050 per share, the September 2026 IPO priced at ₹1,785 through institutional book-building, the stock listed at ₹1,800 and dipped toward ₹1,761 within days. Pre-IPO holders sat in the six-month lock-in and could not sell; IPO allottees could. The entry was above the IPO price before the lock-in even ended.

2. The off-market platforms are not SEBI-recognised. SEBI's advisories (August 2016, December 2024, June 2026) are consistent: platforms facilitating trades in unlisted securities are not authorised or recognised by SEBI, so a trade done through them has no clearing guarantee and no SEBI-administered investor protection. A private unlisted transaction is legal when it complies with company law and the company's articles — but any platform claiming SEBI approval for unlisted share trading should be treated as a red flag. This is the counterparty risk that a listed order book does not carry.

3. There is no single price — only a dealer's ask. No exchange, no order book, no published volume. What you are quoted is a dealer's current ask, which moves on inventory (a dealer with a large position prices to move it), lot size (a 100-share lot is not a 5,000-share lot), IPO news flow (a DRHP filing or a credible listing date moves quotes within hours), and the last funding round (a new round re-anchors the reference valuation). The right question to a counterparty is not "what is the price?" but "at that price, what size can you actually settle, and by when?" — the gap between the quote and the fill is where this market hides its cost.

<2>The tax split that decides what you keep

This is the gap most pages selling pre-IPO shares never state cleanly. Unlisted and pre-IPO equity is taxed differently from listed equity, and the two holding tests do not line up:

Listed equity (STT paid)Unlisted / pre-IPO
Long-term holding,12 months,24 monthsLTCG rate,12.5% (112A), above the shared ₹1.25L pool,12.5% (112), no indexation, NO poolShort-term rate,20% (111A),Your slab rateSTT on the transfer,Yes,No (off-market)

Three consequences that actually change your plan:

  • An unlisted long-term gain (held 24 months+) is 12.5% with no ₹1.25L exemption. The pool that

shelters your listed gains does not apply here. The after-tax wedge article uses the same 12.5% number, but the pool is what separates the two.

  • An unlisted short-term gain (held 24 months or less) is at your slab, not a flat 20%. For a

top-slab investor that is a materially different (worse) outcome — which is why a short holding period is rarely the right plan for a pre-IPO position.

  • Sequence matters. The holding clock starts at your acquisition transfer, not at listing.

Hold unlisted for 18 months, then sell four months after listing on the exchange: you are past 24 months in total, but the sale is now of a listed share, so the 12-month 112A test applies. Take advice on your specific sequence.

When to say no: the IPO may not happen (DRHPs lapse and get withdrawn), the IPO may price below your entry (public-market comparables have been the more conservative number more than once), the platform is unrecognised and off-market, and the "price" is a thin dealer ask with no comparable fills. Two of those four at the same time is enough.
